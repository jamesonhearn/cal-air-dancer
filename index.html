Air Dancer

Simulating an inflatable tube man with airflow-driven cloth

Summary and Team

We will extend the HW4 cloth simulator into a fabric tube with a wrap-around seam, pinned base, and open top, supplied by a fan, to simulate an inflatable tube man. The flow resistance inside the tube will depend on the cloth's shape, so a kink can restrict airflow, change pressure support, and potentially produce collapse and recovery. We will test whether this airflow–cloth coupling is enough to produce the familiar "dance" under a steady fan setting.

Problem Description

Context. An air dancer is a fabric tube on a fan that can collapse and spring back up without a programmed motion controller. A plausible explanation is a feedback loop between the tube's shape and the air inside it (Figure 1). Lab experiments on soft tubes under constant airflow demonstrate kink-driven self-oscillation [4].

Why it matters. Simple cloth inflation models use a single pressure for the whole object [5], and wind models push on the outside. Neither captures air flowing through a deformable tube, which also matters for windsocks, hoses, and inflatables. A Spring 2022 CS184 team attempted a tube man and switched to a windsock instead [6].

Why it's challenging. HW4 only builds flat sheets, so we need a wrap-around seam while keeping the top open. Air pressure must respond to flow and the cloth's changing volume, and the fan can't supply unlimited pressure. Collapse involves tight folds where fabric can pass through itself [3], and stiff pressure forces can make the simulation unstable.

General approach. We split the tube into a chain of air sections. Each step, we estimate the passage cross-section between sections and use it to set how easily air flows through. Each section will track air mass and changing volume using a simple pressure–volume approximation. Flow between sections will conserve air mass, with a bounded fan pressure–flow relationship at the base and an open outlet to ambient air. Pressure differences across the fabric will act alongside gravity, inertia, and cloth forces, allowing us to test whether pressurization supports reopening after a fold.

We will compare this with one uniform internal pressure computed from total air mass and tube volume, and with section pressures whose flow resistance is fixed at its undeformed value. Both controls will retain the same fan and outlet models. These comparisons test which ingredients matter within our implementation and the settings we explore, not which ingredients all real air dancers require.

Goals and Deliverables

Our goal is a cloth tube that collapses and re-inflates repeatedly under a steady fan setting, and evidence of how shape-dependent airflow affects that motion. If the tube doesn't dance, a well-measured negative result will establish the limitations of this implementation under the tested settings.

What we will create

  • Interactive demo: the simulator running live, with sliders for fan setting and fabric stiffness, and a toggle between our model and the simpler ones so viewers can compare how the motion changes.
  • Images and video: side-by-side clips of each model under matched settings. If sustained cycles occur, we will include a filmstrip of one collapse-and-recover cycle (Figure 2).

Questions we plan to answer

  1. Can shape-dependent airflow coupled to cloth dynamics produce repeated collapse and recovery under a steady fan setting?
  2. How does the motion change with simpler models (uniform pressure, or resistance fixed at its undeformed value)?
  3. How does the motion change with fan setting and fabric stiffness?

How we will measure it

  • Sustained motion: number of collapse cycles and whether their size holds steady or dies out after startup. We will choose an observation duration and classification criteria before the main comparisons.
  • Mechanism: pressure, flow, and passage area over time alongside motion, to check whether restriction, loss of support, and recovery occur in the hypothesized sequence.
  • Behavior map: a grid of fan setting × stiffness runs, labeled steady, dying out, repeating, or irregular, with invalid or numerically unstable runs flagged separately.
  • Reliability: sensitivity to a smaller time step and separate refinement of the cloth mesh and air-section resolution while preserving physical parameters.
  • Performance: frame rate and simulated time per wall-clock second vs. mesh resolution; we're aiming for real-time advancement at around 30 fps at a modest resolution, not claiming an achieved benchmark.

Why we think we can do it. The cloth, integration, and collision code come from HW4. The new pieces (a tube mesh, a small air model, and pressure forces) can each be tested on their own, and the air model can be checked on a rigid tube before it touches cloth. Stable coupling and sustained oscillation remain core risks.

Fair comparisons. We will use the same cloth parameters, fan relationship, outlet, initial conditions, and numerical settings across models. Only the pressure-distribution or resistance assumption will change.
